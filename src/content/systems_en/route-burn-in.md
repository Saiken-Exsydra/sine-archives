**Route burn-in** occurs when a Redactory route is repeated so often, or held under such sustained pressure, that continuation becomes partly involuntary.

Any route can burn. A medical support, warming route, lifting pattern, concealment habit, workshop tool, or household convenience can become dangerous if it is kept open too long or trained past the point where release remains a fully conscious act.

## From Fluency to Burn-In

Experienced Redactors develop **Fluency**: familiar steps compress, prepared transitions become faster, and a route can be held beneath conversation or other work. Fluency is not an injury. It is an ordinary result of mastery while release remains clean.

The warning sequence begins when efficiency is followed by loss of clean release:

- **release lag** — the expression continues briefly after the Redactor means to stop;
- **reflexive reopening** — a cue, posture, stressor, or familiar need starts the route without a complete deliberate entry;
- **baseline incorporation** — body and self-model begin treating active routing as an expected condition;
- **burn-in** — continuation has become an involuntary demand on the route capacity and bandwidth required to keep it active.

The stages do not always appear in order, and a practitioner can conceal early symptoms by becoming better at compensating for them.

## What Burn-In Is Not

Burn-in is not stable reconfiguration. If an effect must remain actively routed, it remains instanced even when the Redactor experiences it as normal.

It is also distinct from **Overreach**, **Bruising**, **Fray**, **Sinking**, and **Drowning**. Those conditions can accompany a burned route or make it more dangerous, but they are not alternate names for burn-in.

Nor is burn-in proof of a superior Rank or a specially compatible Anchor. The burned route may be narrow, mundane, or poorly fitted. What has changed is the practitioner's relationship to stopping it.

## Suppression Is Not Route-Breaking

Sedation, unconsciousness, exhaustion, illness, jamming, or another condition that makes support impossible can suppress a route's current expression. That does not erase the learned demand to continue. When the Redactor can support it again, the route may reopen at the capacity available.

A Point can refuse its own mediated action under its defined safety conditions. It does not close the Redactor's Dive, and there is no universal Point or Leaf switch that breaks a route.

## Why It Is Dangerous

An involuntary route still occupies the capacity and bandwidth needed to maintain it. It can narrow recovery, crowd out other expressions, and make a familiar response appear safer than it is. The condition is especially serious when the route supports breathing, circulation, pain control, mobility, body structure, or another necessary function.

Abrupt interruption can expose the untreated condition beneath the support or create a separate release crisis. The appropriate response depends on what the route currently supplies, not on whether it looks dramatic from outside.

## Care and Route-Breaking

Care begins by distinguishing what the route supplies from what the body, environment, or repaired object can carry without it. Clinicians reduce load, build ordinary or stably reconfigured support where possible, identify cues, and restore deliberate interruption.

True **route-breaking** requires the Redactor's participation. It cannot be completed by taking away a Point or ordering a person to stop. When the route sustains a necessary function, care must also plan for replacement support and a consent-bounded release. VÆstar clinicians and technical Redactors work with the patient on the risks each step can safely carry.

## The Cultural Rule

Mature Redactor culture treats burn-in as an occupational injury, not a moral failure. Its most widely repeated warning is also the simplest:

> **Anything permitted to become reflex may eventually act before judgment.**
