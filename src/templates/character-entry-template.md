## Overview

<!--
Goal: answer "Who is this person, what are they like, and why should I care?"

Recommended length: 3-6 short paragraphs.

The overview should normally establish, in this order:
- the person's name, age/life stage, and present role;
- one-sentence local explanations for any unavoidable setting terms;
- their dominant first impression;
- the important correction beneath that first impression;
- the central event, conflict, contradiction, or question shaping their current life.

Do NOT open with succession law, registry minutiae, cosmological classification,
organizational history, or a pile of titles unless one of those is genuinely the
simplest way to understand the person.
-->

## Personality and Presence

<!--
Describe temperament before reputation.

Separate:
- what the person is naturally like;
- what strangers usually see;
- what trusted people see;
- acquired defenses or professional masks;
- habits, humor, anger, affection, fear, aesthetics, and social behavior.

If the character changed after a major event, make the before/after distinction explicit
instead of presenting the later defense as though it were always their personality.
-->

## Background

<!--
Give the minimum history needed to make the present character make sense.

Use causal structure:
1. what their life was like before the formative event or condition;
2. what happened;
3. what it cost;
4. what changed afterward;
5. what remains unresolved now.

If one event divides the character's life cleanly, give that event its own H2 section
instead of burying it inside a general biography dump.
-->

## Practice and Abilities

<!--
Teach the reader the character's actual method before cataloguing mechanics.

On first use, briefly explain setting-specific terms such as Redactor, Anchor, Dive,
Pathway, Mark, Bloom, Calling, Shore, etc. Only explain the piece needed here; link to
the full system article for the rest when an appropriate public entry exists.

Cover:
- what the ability looks like in ordinary use;
- what the character prefers to do with it;
- recognizable visual or behavioral signatures;
- why their personal technique fits their temperament/history;
- what makes them distinct from another practitioner of the same system.

Do not write an ability as a list of nouns with no scene-level meaning.
-->

## Signature Technique or Fighting Style

<!-- OPTIONAL.
Use when the character has a specific craft, apparatus, weapon, schematic, ritual,
combat doctrine, or other technique that deserves more space than the general ability
section. Explain the governing idea first, then the components.

A small list or table is appropriate only when it genuinely makes a multi-part technique
easier to understand. The prose before it must explain why the parts belong together.
-->

## Limits and Risks

<!--
State costs and failure modes in the language the reader already learned above.
Distinguish "cannot" from "can, but at a cost" from "can, but refuses because of risk."

Include psychological or institutional limits only when they causally change what the
character can do. Avoid fake balance language added merely to make a powerful character
sound constrained.
-->

## Place in the World

<!--
Only now expand the institutional frame: House, corporation, academy, church, military,
political office, social class, professional standing, succession, legal status, etc.

Explain what each affiliation means for THIS person. Do not turn the section into an
organization codex. If the reader needs the organization's full history, that belongs
on the organization's page.
-->

## Important Relationships

<!--
Use short H3 subsections for relationships that actually alter the character.

For each relationship, answer some combination of:
- who the other person is to them;
- what the relationship feels like in practice;
- what each person wants or fears from the other;
- what history complicates the bond;
- what the character cannot easily say aloud.

Do not reduce relationships to genealogy or titles.
-->

## Restricted Record / Deeper Lore

<!--
Final layer. Put spoiler-heavy, classified, cosmological, identity-level, or unresolved
material here after the reader has the vocabulary needed to understand it.

The section should still be explanatory, not a raw dossier paste.

Recommended order:
1. state the simple correction to the public understanding;
2. explain the hidden history in plain language;
3. explain the deeper mechanic or cosmology only as it affects the character;
4. distinguish common misreadings explicitly ("not possession," "not resurrection," etc.);
5. end on the character-level consequence, not on a taxonomy label.
